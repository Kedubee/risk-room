Compound: learning objectives and a short teacher's guide

What this guide is

This guide is for secondary-school teachers of geography, science and civics who teach learners aged 15–18. It sets out ten learning objectives for Compound, a free browser game about disaster risk reduction (DRR),1 and a one-session plan. For the UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), DRR is aimed at preventing new disaster risk, reducing existing risk and managing the risk that remains (entry "Disaster risk reduction").2

The annex maps the objectives to two documents: Towards a Learning Culture of Safety and Resilience, by UNESCO and UNICEF (2014),3 and UNESCO's Education for Sustainable Development Goals: Learning Objectives (ESD for SDGs, 2017).4 The annex lists what the game does not teach.

About the game

⌨

Controls. Students move a small robot, the player, with WASD or the arrow keys; aiming and firing are automatic, and Space or the EVAC button dashes out of the way. Touch screens work: drag anywhere to move. There is no pause button. The game plays sound: M mutes it (on a touch screen, the ♫ button).

⚠

Hazards. By design, slimes, golems and other figures are hazard impacts, not creatures: destroying one stands for absorbing what gets through. Five events, such as a tsunami, cannot be destroyed: each is marked on the ground, with a warning line, before it lands; the player can only dodge. Cracks and burn marks are scenery, not warnings. From the second minute come compound events, "Several hazards, arriving together."; later, a bigger one, "the boss" (on Planned, at four minutes).

◉

Three terms (UNDRR's, paraphrased).2 Exposure: the situation of people and assets in hazard-prone areas (entry "Exposure"), like standing in a tsunami's marked lane. Vulnerability: conditions that make people, communities, assets or systems more susceptible to hazards' impacts (entry "Vulnerability"); Retrofitting lowers it. Capacity: the strengths and resources available to manage and reduce disaster risk (entry "Capacity"); in the game, simplified to the red bar, which hits drain.

⚑

Places. The start screen offers all six place types, in fixed order, each listing its hazards and start times, under "Risk is local." "Same place again" skips it, replaying the same place and hazard order; the other end-of-run buttons return to it. The start screen's "Art credits" names who made most pictures.

✚

Lifelines, services such as hospitals, are three or four per run. A damaged one repairs while the player stands in its ring, fastest once nothing sits on it; under several hazards it can keep losing health even then. A run ends when the red bar empties or too many lifelines are lost (on Planned, all of them).

♣

Cards. Destroyed hazards drop blue gems (How to play, with older pictures, says "blue dots"); walking near them fills the blue bar. Each level-up pauses play for a card choice; the ten measures are cards. The Sources switch there, also on the start screen, shows each card's source, if any. Two measures that go together can bring a synergy card offering more. From level 4, a trade-off card may offer a gain at a stated cost; while a lifeline is lost, one to rebuild it comes at every level-up.

⚙

Settings. Under "How fast does this place grow?", the start screen offers four steps: Planned, Growing, Booming, Unchecked. This guide recommends Planned, which the game says starts gentler, then gets hard quickly; "Plain English" (below the steps) on, for plainer wording in many cards and messages; Sources on. A first visit starts on Growing, Plain English on, Sources off; the browser remembers all three, progress and the help seen (How to play and hints open by themselves once per browser). On a used computer, open How to play.

✓

After the run. First, up to four questions: on two measures combined, an event met, and field questions (hazard judgements, some place-specific). In 60 of 64 event and field questions the longest option is right: assess notes and discussion, not scores. Then the report, mostly click-to-open sections, including "What you built", "Lifelines", "Measures that compound" (every run) and "What you could only dodge" (once an event is met). Any end-of-run button closes it.

Learning objectives

The objectives are written in this guide's own words, in the three parts of Towards, Box 10: knowledge and understanding, skills, and attitudes/dispositions (§3.3, p. 50).3 Each names what carries it. No objective promises a change in feelings, values or behaviour from playing alone.

Knowledge and understanding

  1. 1

    Learners will explain that disaster risk depends on the hazard and also on exposure, vulnerability and the capacity to cope, and that risk can be reduced by measures that work on these, even where the hazard itself cannot be stopped, as with the events in the game.

    Carried by: the game in some runs, and the class discussion.

  2. 2

    Learners will describe several of the game's ten risk-reduction measures (building codes, early warning, drills, green defences, retrofitting, risk data, risk financing, land-use planning, maintenance and resilient investment) and say what each of those does.

    Carried by: the game, in any run of at least two minutes.

  3. 3

    Learners will explain why the lifelines their run places (three or four of these: hospitals, schools, power, water and road links) matter during and after a disaster.

    Carried by: the game, and the classroom activity (the report's Lifelines section).

  4. 4

    Learners will explain the argument that reducing risk before a disaster is more cost-effective than relying mainly on response and recovery afterwards, and that some choices bring a gain and a cost at the same time.

    Carried by: the game in some runs, and the class discussion.

  5. 5

    Learners will name the main hazards where they live and compare them with the hazards of the game place most like their own area, among the six places the start screen offers.

    Carried by: the classroom activity.

  6. 6

    Learners will recognise that a disaster does not affect everyone equally, and that warnings and drills can miss some people.

    Carried by: the game in some runs, and the closing task.

Skills

  1. 7

    Learners will read the warning signs the game shows before an event and act on them in time.

    Carried by: the game, in any run of at least two minutes.

  2. 8

    Learners will explain that two measures that go together can do more than either does alone, and identify how hazards that arrive together make the harm grow.

    Carried by: the game in some runs, and the class discussion.

  3. 9

    Learners will use the end-of-run report to say what they protected, what they lost and why, and to choose a different strategy for a second run and compare the two.

    Carried by: the game (the report) and the classroom activity (the comparison).

Attitudes/dispositions

  1. 10

    Learners will reflect on the values behind their choices: what they chose to protect, what they left unfunded, and who would pay for that in a real place.

    Carried by: the class discussion; the game supplies the material.

How to use this guide

Objectives 2 and 7 need only the game (any run of at least two minutes), 5 and 10 only the activities below, and the rest both. Neither document sets a level for ages 15–18. Towards gives ages 14–18 only as an indicative example (Table 3, pp. 70–71).3 ESD for SDGs says "educators and curriculum developers must define the level to be achieved by their learners" (p. 8).4

This guide recommends one 45–60-minute session, students alone or in pairs. Its stages and timings adapt Lesson 1 of UNDRR's teacher's guide for another DRR game, Stop Disasters (p. 6).5

A note on sensitivity

Some students may have lived through a disaster, directly or through family. The Stop Disasters guide's note on trauma sensitivity (p. 38),5 applied here:

  • Beforehand, consider whether any student might need extra support.
  • Where possible, discuss what communities can plan and do, not what individuals suffered; the game casts students as planners.
  • Let any student opt out, without a reason, of activities about their own place, experience or values (Before play, Discussion, Closing task).
  • Notice any student who seems upset, or much quieter than usual.
  • Ask the school's safeguarding or counselling staff about any concern.

Annex: how the objectives map to the two documents

Strength rule. An objective is strong when both of these hold; otherwise it is partial.

(1) Carried by is Game alone: the game shows it on screen in every run that lasts at least two minutes, without the player opening anything, through the start screen, the cards, the events, the lifelines or the end-of-run report. A collapsed section of the report counts by its title, not by its content; the start screen does not count for a run started with "Same place again", which skips it. Game, some runs (an element that appears only in some runs) and Class (a classroom activity), alone or together with Game, do not meet this test.

(2) The Towards Box 10 line it rests on comes from the same part of Box 10 as the objective's type: a knowledge objective from Knowledge and understanding, a skills objective from Skills, an attitudes objective from Attitudes/dispositions. No line mapped does not meet this test.

The threshold is two minutes because any run that lasts that long has, by then, met its first events and its first compound event.

The ESD for SDGs column4 is shown for reference only. It does not change the strength. In that column, SDG 11, cognitive 2 means the second objective in the cognitive domain (knowledge and thinking skills, p. 11) of the ESD for SDGs table for Sustainable Development Goal 11; behavioural objectives describe action.

Strong means coverage, not attainment: the game shows the objective in every run of at least two minutes and its Box 10 line comes from the matching part, which does not show that learners reach the outcome; for objective 7, the warnings read in every run are the game's own stylised ground marks and warning lines, not the real-world signs and signals the Box 10 line names.

Gaps: what the two documents list that the game does not carry

  • Attitudes, values and action. Box 10 has a whole attitudes/dispositions part, and the ESD for SDGs objectives have a socio-emotional domain and a behavioural domain ("The behavioural domain describes action competencies.", p. 11). The game carries almost none of this. Objective 10 and the closing task are classroom work only.
  • Real drills. Box 10 includes the outcome "Learners have the skills set necessary for participating in early warning and evacuation drills" (p. 54). The game shows why drills matter; it does not train a drill.
  • Climate science. The first three SDG 13 cognitive objectives (the greenhouse effect and the human causes of climate change; Table 1.2.13, p. 36) are not in the game.
  • The learner's own place. The six places are types, not real places. Objective 5 needs the teacher to bring in local hazards.
  • Equity. Only a few of the game's questions deal with who is left out (objective 6).
  • Age level. Neither document sets a level for ages 15–18 (see How to use this guide).
